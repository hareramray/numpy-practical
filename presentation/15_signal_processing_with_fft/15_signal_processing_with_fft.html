Welcome to notebook 15. A sampled signal is just a 1D array of values recorded at evenly spaced times. The fast Fourier transform, np.fft, rewrites that array as a sum of sine waves of different frequencies, which makes it easy to see which frequencies are present and to remove the ones you do not want. The only extra prerequisite is knowing that a sine wave repeats at a frequency measured in cycles per second, or hertz. The notebook imports its own libraries, including matplotlib for the three plots you will see.

Step 1 · Build a signal

A signal is an array of samples

Sample rate fs = 256

256 evenly spaced samples cover one second.

Nyquist limit fs / 2

Above 128 Hz, frequencies cannot be uniquely recovered.

Known ingredients

A 5 Hz sine plus a 40 Hz sine with amplitude 0.4.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
sample_rate = 256
time = np.arange(sample_rate) / sample_rate
signal = (np.sin(2 * np.pi * 5 * time)
          + 0.4 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 40 * time))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3))
ax.plot(time, signal)
plt.show()
assert signal.shape == (256,)

3

Sampling records a continuous signal at evenly spaced times. With a sample rate fs of 256 per second, time is 0, 1/256, 2/256 and so on, up to but not including 1 second, so a one-second record contains exactly fs samples without repeating the endpoint; the notebook calls this the endpoint=False idea. The signal is a NumPy expression evaluated on the whole time array at once: a 5 Hz sine plus a 40 Hz sine scaled by 0.4. The Nyquist frequency, fs / 2 = 128 Hz, is the highest frequency this sampling can represent without ambiguity. The plot is on the next slide. Try it: change the 40 Hz amplitude, say to 0.8, and predict the effect: the fast ripple gets taller in the time plot, and its spike in the spectrum grows to 0.8.

Step 2 · Time into frequency

rfft finds the frequency peaks

spectrum = np.fft.rfft(signal)
frequencies = np.fft.rfftfreq(signal.size,
                              d=1 / sample_rate)
amplitude = np.abs(spectrum) / signal.size
# Double only bins with negative-frequency partners
amplitude[1:-1] *= 2
peak_indices = np.argsort(amplitude)[-2:][::-1]
print("Peak frequencies:", frequencies[peak_indices])
print("Peak amplitudes:", amplitude[peak_indices])

Peak frequencies: [ 5. 40.]
Peak amplitudes: [1.  0.4]

rfft for real input

Only the nonnegative frequencies, 0 to fs/2.

Bins are fs/N apart

256 / 256 = 1 Hz per bin.

Scale to amplitude

Divide by N, then double the paired bins.

5

For a real-valued signal, np.fft.rfft returns one complex coefficient per nonnegative frequency; the negative frequencies would only mirror them. np.fft.rfftfreq gives the frequency of each coefficient when you pass the sample spacing d = 1 / fs, and the spacing between bins is fs / N, here exactly 1 Hz. The magnitude divided by N, with every bin except 0 Hz and the Nyquist bin doubled because each stands for a positive and a negative frequency, recovers the amplitudes of the sines: 1 at 5 Hz and 0.4 at 40 Hz. The record holds whole cycles of both sines, so each lands exactly on one bin. Try it: use 5.5 Hz instead of 5 Hz. Now 5.5 falls between bins, and its energy leaks into nearby bins, so the spike becomes a smaller, wider bump; windowing is the usual remedy.

Step 3 · Filter and reconstruct

Zero the bins, then invert

Copy, then mask

Bins above 10 Hz become 0; the 5 Hz bin stays.

irfft returns to time

Pass n=signal.size to keep the length, even for odd N.

The asserts pass

Filtered equals the pure 5 Hz sine within atol 1e-12.

filtered_spectrum = spectrum.copy()
filtered_spectrum[frequencies > 10] = 0
filtered = np.fft.irfft(filtered_spectrum,
                        n=signal.size)
expected = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 5 * time)
np.testing.assert_allclose(filtered, expected,
                           atol=1e-12)
np.testing.assert_allclose(
    np.fft.irfft(spectrum, n=signal.size),
    signal, atol=1e-12)

7

Filtering in the frequency domain takes three moves. Copy the spectrum so the original stays intact. Use the boolean mask frequencies > 10 to set every coefficient above 10 Hz to zero, which removes the 40 Hz component. Then np.fft.irfft converts the edited spectrum back into samples; passing n=signal.size guarantees the original length, which matters for odd-length signals. The first assert proves the result is the pure 5 Hz sine, and the second proves that the unedited spectrum round-trips to the original signal. This is an ideal cutoff, fine for a learning example; for other signals an abrupt cutoff can cause ringing, and real filter design also considers boundaries and transients. Try it: set the cutoff to 50 Hz. Both 5 Hz and 40 Hz now survive, so irfft recovers the original signal.

The idea to remember

One signal,
two views.

The FFT rewrites samples as frequencies. Edit the frequencies, invert, and you are back in time.

Pause here. A signal can be described by its values over time or by the strength of each frequency it contains, and np.fft moves between the two without losing information. Some questions are easy in one view and hard in the other: which frequencies are present and how strong, or how to remove one of them, are easy in frequency. Remember the practical details: rfft for real input, rfftfreq with d = 1 / fs for the axis, and irfft with n to come back at the right length.

The challenge has no supplied check: decide what you expect first. Create the noise with a seeded generator, for example np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 0.3, size), so your results are reproducible. RMSE is the square root of the mean squared difference from the clean sine. Before filtering, the RMSE is roughly the noise level; after zeroing bins above a cutoff just above the sine's frequency, most of the broadband noise is removed and the RMSE should drop, while the sine survives. A cutoff below the sine's frequency would remove the signal itself, and a very high cutoff keeps most of the noise, so the right cutoff depends on where the useful frequencies are.
