Step 1 · Mean, median, and spread

Five summaries of five deliveries

delivery_minutes = np.array([20., 22., 21., 23., 90.])
print("Mean:", delivery_minutes.mean())
print("Median:", np.median(delivery_minutes))
print("Population std:", delivery_minutes.std(ddof=0))
print("Sample std:", delivery_minutes.std(ddof=1))
print("Quartiles:", np.percentile(delivery_minutes,
                                [25, 50, 75]))

Mean: 35.2
Median: 22.0
Population std: 27.41824210265859
Sample std: 30.65452658254568
Quartiles: [21. 22. 23.]

Mean vs median

One 90 lifts the mean to 35.2; the middle value stays 22.

ddof=0 or ddof=1

Variance divided by N, or by N − 1 to estimate from a sample.

Percentiles

The 25th, 50th and 75th are the quartiles: 21, 22, 23.

3

Four deliveries took about 20 minutes and one took 90. The mean adds everything and divides by five, so the single slow delivery drags it up to 35.2. The median sorts the values and takes the middle one, 22, which ignores how extreme the 90 is. Standard deviation measures typical distance from the mean. NumPy's default ddof=0 divides the variance by N; ddof=1 divides by N minus 1, the usual choice when estimating from a sample, so it is slightly larger: 30.65 versus 27.42. Percentiles mark positions in the sorted data; the quartiles 21, 22 and 23 show that the middle half is tightly packed. Try it: replace 90 with 24. The mean falls from 35.2 to 22.0, while the median stays at 22.0.

Step 2 · Reduce the intended axis

One table, two different totals

sales = np.array([[5, 3, 2], [7, 2, 4],
                  [4, 6, 3], [8, 4, 5]])
products = np.array(["tea", "coffee", "juice"])
daily = sales.sum(axis=1)
product_totals = sales.sum(axis=0)
print("Daily:", daily)
print("Product totals:", product_totals)
print("Top product:", products[product_totals.argmax()])
assert daily.sum() == product_totals.sum()

Daily: [10 13 13 17]
Product totals: [24 15 14]
Top product: tea

axis=1: per day

Reduces the product axis, leaving one total per row.

axis=0: per product

Reduces the day axis, leaving one total per column.

argmax vs max

argmax returns a position, 0, which labels the winner: tea.

NumPy course · 06 Statistics and aggregation

5

The table has four days as rows and three products as columns, so its shape is (4, 3). Two different questions use the same data. Totals per day reduce the product axis, axis=1, giving four numbers. Totals per product reduce the day axis, axis=0, giving three numbers. A helpful phrase: the axis you pass is the axis that disappears. max would tell you the largest total, 24; argmax tells you where it is, position 0, which we use to index the product names. The assert confirms both ways of adding give the same grand total. Try it: sales.argmax(axis=0) finds the best day for each product, giving positions [3 2 3], that is, the fourth day for tea and juice and the third day for coffee.

Step 3 · Weighted and cumulative summaries

Weights, running totals, changes

marks = np.array([70., 80., 90.])
weights = np.array([0.2, 0.3, 0.5])
print("Weighted grade:",
      np.average(marks, weights=weights))
daily_sales = np.array([100., 150., 120., 180.])
print("Running total:", np.cumsum(daily_sales))
print("Changes:", np.diff(daily_sales))
np.testing.assert_allclose(
    np.average(marks, weights=weights), 83.)

Weighted grade: 83.0
Running total: [100. 250. 370. 550.]
Changes: [ 50. -30.  60.]

Weighted mean

0.2 × 70 + 0.3 × 80 + 0.5 × 90 = 83. Weights need a nonzero sum.

cumsum: running total

Each entry is the total so far.

diff: the changes

Next minus previous, so the result is one entry shorter.

NumPy course · 06 Statistics and aggregation

7

np.average with weights lets observations count differently. The final exam weighs 0.5, so the grade, 83, sits closer to 90 than the plain mean of 80 would. NumPy divides by the sum of the weights, which therefore must not be zero. np.cumsum keeps a running total: 100, then 100 + 150 = 250, then 370, then 550. np.diff subtracts each value from the next: +50, −30, +60. Four days give three changes, because diff shortens the axis by one. Try it: compute a running mean by dividing np.cumsum(daily_sales) by np.arange(1, n + 1), where n is the number of days. Here that gives 100, 125, about 123.33, and 137.5.

8

Each bar is one entry of np.cumsum(daily_sales). The blue part is the running total carried over from the previous day, and the orange part is the new day's sales stacked on top, so the bar heights climb 100, 250, 370, 550. A cumulative sum never goes down while the inputs are positive; that is why it suits running revenue. np.diff answers the opposite question, how much each day differs from the one before: 150 minus 100 is 50, 120 minus 150 is −30, 180 minus 120 is 60. Notice diff of the cumulative sum gives back the daily sales from day two onwards: the two operations undo each other.

The idea to remember

Name the axis
that disappears.

sum, mean and argmax collapse the axis you pass. Decide the question first, then check the shape that is left.

Pause here. Almost every summary in NumPy is a reduction along an axis. Before typing axis=0 or axis=1, say the question out loud, such as "one total per product", and ask which dimension has to vanish to answer it. Then check the result's shape: four days and three products should give (3,) for per-product totals. And when outliers are present, prefer the median and percentiles to the mean and standard deviation.
